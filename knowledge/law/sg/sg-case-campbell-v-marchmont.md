## Text

Summary: a landlord served several notices of breach and notices of termination on a hotel tenant within five months of a three-year lease, kept taking rent 'without prejudice', and eventually sued for possession. The Appellate Division adopted a more landlord-friendly approach to the notice of breach requirement in s 18(1) CLPA (a notice is valid for any breach that is properly particularised, even if others are not), held that the landlord had not waived its right of forfeiture after January 2022, but held the tenancy ended only when the landlord started and served its court claim, so double rent for holding over ran only from the next day (s 28(4) Civil Law Act 1909). One of the issues was whether the tenant should get relief against forfeiture under s 18(3) CLPA; the outcome on that point was not checked for this entry.

At [99]: "Marchmont did not validly determine the tenancy by exercising its right of forfeiture until the commencement and service of OC 492 on 28 December 2022."

## In plain words

Sending a notice of breach and a notice of termination did not by itself end the tenancy in this case. The tenancy ended only when the landlord actually started and served the court claim for possession. A landlord who keeps accepting rent while threatening to end the tenancy can raise a waiver argument, although here it failed. The case also shows that notices have to be specific about the breach.

## When it matters in an NDA or service agreement

For a shop-lot or commercial tenancy the case is a useful reality check on clauses that say the tenancy ends automatically, or that the landlord may re-enter immediately, on a short rent delay. It shows that, in Singapore, ending a lease for breach is a process with notice and, in practice, a court claim. It concerns a large commercial lease; do not read it as a rule for small residential rentals. General information, not legal advice.
