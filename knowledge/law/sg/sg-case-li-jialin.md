## Text

Summary: a developer purported to forfeit a deposit of about 63% of the price of an apartment, then reduced the forfeiture to 20% and withheld more as damages. The Court of Appeal held that the reasonableness of a forfeitable deposit is judged when the contract is made, not when it is forfeited, so a deposit that was unreasonable at the start cannot be forfeited even in part. It also held that the penalty rule does not apply to a true (earnest) deposit.

At [3]: "If it is not reasonable at the time of contracting, it is not a true deposit and therefore not subject to forfeiture. It does not become reasonable retrospectively by the relevant party purporting to forfeit a reduced sum. In short, the question is not how much one can forfeit, but whether one can forfeit at all."

## In plain words

A deposit that a party can keep if the other side defaults must be a reasonable amount when the deal is signed. If it is too large to be reasonable, the court will not let the party keep a smaller slice of it; the deposit is simply not forfeitable. This is a sale-of-property case, so it applies to tenancies only by analogy.

## When it matters in an NDA or service agreement

A very large deposit is a flag in itself. For a tenancy, ask how the deposit compares with the monthly rent and the landlord's realistic exposure (the playbook sets out the usual range), and whether it can be kept 'at the landlord's discretion'. Whether this reasoning would carry over to a tenant's security deposit is for a court to decide; Equation Recycling (sg-case-equation-recycling) is the closest local decision found.
