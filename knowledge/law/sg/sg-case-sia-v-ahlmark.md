## Text

Summary: a pilot signed a training agreement promising liquidated damages (on a sliding scale, secured by a bank guarantee) if he left or was dismissed within five years of training, including if his services were 'terminated for any reason whatsoever'. The airline ended his employment on three months' pay in lieu of notice and claimed the damages. The High Court dismissed the airline's appeal: it could not claim them.

At [27]: "It would be utterly absurd if the company could terminate without cause and then claim to be entitled to liquidated damages from the trainee."

At [29]: "As the clause works to the financial detriment of a contracting party, the circumstances in which it is to operate must be stated with sufficient precision. It is not good enough to leave them to conjecture."

At [30]: "The company is clearly not entitled to claim damages, liquidated or otherwise, from the plaintiff."

## In plain words

A training bond must say clearly when it applies. If the employer can end the job without any fault of the employee and still demand the bond money, a court may refuse to enforce it. Here the employer chose to terminate on notice and pay, and then could not also treat the termination as a trigger for the bond.

## When it matters in an NDA or service agreement

Check any bond, training-cost repayment or 'liquidated damages if you leave or are dismissed' clause. Ones that apply to dismissal for any reason, including redundancy or termination without cause, are a red flag. Ask for repayment to be limited to resignation (or dismissal for the employee's own misconduct), to the real cost, and reduced over time. A 1999 first-instance case on one contract's wording; it does not decide whether every bond is valid.
