## Text

Summary: a former senior sales employee was sued over restraints in his employment letter and confidentiality agreement. His 12-month non-solicitation and non-compete clauses were found to protect legitimate interests (a stable trained workforce and trade connections) but were held unreasonable and unenforceable, and could not be saved by severance. A one-year restraint period was accepted as reasonable in principle.

At [41]: "Therefore, I find that the plaintiff has not shown that the Non-solicitation Obligation was reasonable as between the parties. It follows that the Non-solicitation Obligation is not valid or enforceable."

At [42]: "the prohibition on any employment or engagement which “by the nature of the employment or engagement will be detrimental or may be perceived to be detrimental to the best interest of the Company” goes well beyond what is necessary to protect the plaintiff’s interest in maintaining its trade connection."

At [45]: "the “notional severance” approach – whereby the court is entitled not just to delete the parties’ words but also to alter or even insert words – has not been accepted into Singapore law"

## In plain words

Two common clauses failed. A promise not to solicit 'any employee' reached people the departing manager had no influence over and who were not hard to replace. A non-compete that barred any job 'perceived' as harmful to the employer's profit or standing was far wider than protecting client relationships. A twelve-month period itself was fine. The court would not rewrite either clause, and the 'blue pencil' (striking out words) could not leave a workable version.

## When it matters in an NDA or service agreement

Look for non-solicit clauses that cover every employee rather than the ones the person worked with, and for non-competes defined by loose words such as 'detrimental', 'competitive' or 'any business of the Group'. The more tightly a clause is tied to the person's own clients and team, and to about 6-12 months, the more likely it is to be treated as reasonable. This is one first-instance decision on its facts.
